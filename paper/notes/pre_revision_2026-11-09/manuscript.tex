\title{Handedness for Bangla writer identification: Oracle gains and predicted-attribute utility}
\author[1]{Tanzim Hasan Prappo}
\author[1]{M. Shahidur Rahman}
\address[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Computer Science and Engineering, }\orgname{Shahjalal University of Science and Technology, }\orgaddress{\state{Sylhet 3114, }\country{Bangladesh}}}
\corres{Tanzim Hasan Prappo (\email{2021331006@student.sust.edu}), corresponding author. Coauthor email: \email{rahmanms@sust.edu}.}
\keywords{Writer identification | Soft biometrics | Handedness | Bangla handwriting | Score fusion | Evaluation protocol}
\abstract[ABSTRACT]{Attribute-classification accuracy can misrepresent the identification value of a soft biometric. We present a controlled framework separating oracle gains, prediction errors, evidence dependence and writer-associated information, and apply it to BanglaHand: 42 writers, six representations and 120 identification settings. Normalization uses selected gallery references only; query-handedness models exclude the queried writer from training and calibration. Known handedness improves top-1 accuracy by 6.1 percentage points, compared with 6.9 points for an expected known random partition. With query handedness predicted at 65.5\% accuracy on self-chosen paragraphs, calibrated fixed-weight fusion changes identification accuracy by -0.6 points, properly nested learned weighting by -0.08, and hard filtering by -14.3. Neither fusion rule establishes a gain in the designated comparisons. Filtering and margin diagnostics explain how joint correctness and confidence affect ranking. Random writer labels expose writer-associated information in enrolled-writer attribute evaluation. An external CSAFE evaluation uses 96 writers and separate gallery/query capture sessions. Handedness accuracy is 67.7\% for same-prompt and 58.3\% for cross-prompt session-2 queries; none of eight primary fusion comparisons establishes a Holm-adjusted gain. Session-3 and gallery-subsample analyses provide secondary checks. The framework provides reusable controls for evaluating auxiliary attributes in biometric candidate ranking, helping researchers distinguish oracle opportunity from operational benefit and make fusion decisions using measured identification utility.}

\section{Introduction}
A soft biometric describes a person without uniquely identifying them. Gender, age and handedness can supplement identity matching by changing candidate scores or restricting a gallery \cite{jain2004soft,dantcheva2016soft}. Handwriting studies have predicted such attributes from static images \cite{liwicki2011gender,siddiqi2015gender,bouadjenek2016soft,almaadeed2016handedness,morera2018handedness,rahmanian2021handedness}. The downstream question is different: does the predicted attribute improve identification of the writer?

Two issues complicate this question. First, an incorrect hard attribute decision can exclude the true writer. Its utility depends on identification performance conditional on attribute correctness, not just the marginal accuracy of each system. Soft fusion avoids automatic exclusion, but depends on probability calibration and on whether the attribute scores add information to the identity scores. Both can come from the same image and contain redundant evidence. Second, handwriting carries writer-associated information \cite{bulacu2007text,schomaker2007codebooks}. An attribute predictor evaluated on previously seen writers can exploit that information. Such evaluation may match an enrolment-conditioned application, but does not estimate attribute generalization to unseen writers \cite{kaufman2012leakage}.

The contribution of this study is a controlled framework for evaluating whether predicted soft-biometric information provides downstream identification utility. Separating oracle gains, prediction errors, evidence dependence and writer-associated information shows why attribute-classification accuracy alone can be misleading. Known random partitions benchmark oracle opportunity; calibrated fixed and nested learned-weight fusion measure achieved utility; and joint-correctness, margin and random-label diagnostics distinguish sources of apparent gains. The elementary filtering identity and standard fusion rules are diagnostic tools and comparators.

Building on the companion study \cite{prappo2026handedness}, which examines handedness classification and photometric cues, we reuse BanglaHand \cite{banglahand2026data}, its preprocessing, descriptors and frozen network representations. The present study evaluates whether predicted handedness provides downstream writer-identification utility. Its methodological contribution is a controlled evaluation that separates oracle gains from operational gains through random-partition controls, nested fusion-weight selection, and analyses of error dependence and writer-associated information.

The BanglaHand analysis is complemented by an external CSAFE evaluation of the same utility question on independent writers, separate source pages and capture sessions \cite{crawford2020database,crawford2026csafe}.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work; Section 3 defines the protocol; Section 4 reports the results; and Sections 5--7 discuss their implications, scope and conclusions.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Soft biometrics and downstream utility}
Soft-biometric fusion predates handwriting-based handedness prediction. Jain et al.\ combine demographic information with a primary biometric, while Dantcheva et al.\ distinguish attribute estimation, database indexing and recognition applications \cite{jain2004soft,dantcheva2016soft}. These foundations motivate auxiliary evidence, but its usefulness depends on the available metadata, prediction uncertainty and primary matcher. A correct attribute can narrow a candidate set; a predicted attribute can also demote the true candidate. Consequently, attribute recognition and identification improvement require different endpoints. The present work examines this distinction for handwriting through paired comparisons with the same gallery, identity scores and query samples.

\subsection{Handedness and demographic prediction}
Early handwriting studies investigated gender and other demographic traits using online dynamics, offline shape and texture, and combinations of descriptors \cite{liwicki2011gender,siddiqi2015gender,bouadjenek2016soft,hassaine2013icdar}. Al-Maadeed et al.\ study a 121-writer QUWI subset with Arabic and English documents, combining handcrafted features, fuzzy conceptual reduction and a nearest-neighbour classifier \cite{almaadeed2016handedness}. Their principal evaluation concerns handedness classification and feature reduction. Multiple scripts and text conditions broaden that evaluation, but do not by themselves measure the change in an identity ranking caused by predicted handedness.

Morera et al.\ use CNN word predictions and line-level voting on IAM and KHATT, explicitly separating training and test individuals \cite{morera2018handedness}. They report handedness overall accuracy of 90.70\% on IAM and 70.91\% on KHATT. Their respective left-handed recalls are 63.40\% and 38.64\%, illustrating why overall accuracy must be interpreted alongside class composition and class-specific performance. Rahmanian and Shayegan compare DenseNet201, InceptionV3 and Xception on the same two databases and report 99.14\% KHATT handedness accuracy \cite{rahmanian2021handedness}. This is an attribute-classification result; the accessible publisher record does not establish all details needed for a matched writer-disjoint comparison. We therefore make no inference about writer overlap in that study.

The companion study evaluates writer-independent Bangla handedness classification and photometric cues \cite{prappo2026handedness}. Its 77.4\% balanced accuracy aggregates SVM margins over six shared paragraphs under repeated five-fold evaluation. Here, a calibrated single-query probability modifies a closed-gallery identity ranking under leave-one-writer-out fitting. Different text aggregation, fitting and endpoints prevent a direct numerical comparison. Table~\ref{tab:related} separates these prediction tasks from writer-recognition tasks and the utility endpoint considered here.

\subsection{Writer recognition and evaluation controls}
Text-independent writer recognition models individual writing style through contour directions, hinges, allographs and contour-fragment codebooks \cite{bulacu2007text,schomaker2007codebooks}. Learned representations can target identity directly: FragNet combines feature-pyramid and fragment pathways for word- and page-level writer identification across four benchmarks \cite{he2020fragnet}. In Bangla, Paul et al.\ combine feature extraction and genetic feature selection for writer verification \cite{paul2024bangla}; BanglaWriting and the Boise State collection support related handwriting-recognition research \cite{mridha2021banglawriting,majid2018bangla}. Verification, recognition of written content and closed-set identity ranking address different decisions. Our frozen representations provide controlled comparators, not a claim to outperform handwriting-specialized recognition networks.

Evaluation design also determines what an apparent improvement means. Writer separation addresses generalization to new individuals; it does not alone establish that an attribute supplies information beyond the identity matcher. Leakage analysis requires specifying the information available at each fitting stage \cite{kaufman2012leakage}, and fusion-weight selection must be separated from evaluation to avoid selection bias \cite{cawley2010selection}. We bring these concerns together by measuring oracle opportunity, predicted-attribute utility, error dependence and writer-associated predictability, with an external evaluation on separate CSAFE pages and capture sessions. This combination defines the contribution rather than a new attribute classifier or an accuracy ranking across incompatible protocols.

\section{Materials and methods}
\label{sec:protocol}
\subsection{Queries, references and representations}
BanglaHand contains 336 paragraph photographs from 42 writers (21 left-handed), with common materials and one controlled photographic session \cite{banglahand2026data}. Each writer supplied six shared prompts and two self-chosen texts. A query is a whole paragraph or one of 2, 4 or 8 horizontal strips, approximately 3.5, 1.75 or 0.9 lines. Strip boundaries follow low-ink rows near equally spaced cuts. Of 5040 candidate units, 39 fail the fixed feature-validity rules; exclusion counts and weighting are reported in the supplement. At width 1,536 pixels, rows with ink fraction above 0.01 define a text region through its 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles. Nominal strip cuts move to minima of the row-ink profile smoothed with standard deviation 96/5 pixels. The search half-width is the smaller of 48 pixels and the text-block height divided by $3n$, with a minimum of three pixels. Row fractions transfer the boundaries across image scales. A unit is invalid if its high-resolution cropped mask contains fewer than 200 ink pixels, its 768-pixel mask contains fewer than 50, contour or skeleton extraction fails, or features are nonfinite. All representations use the same validity mask; the 39 excluded units are strips, with no paragraph or writer removed. Photographs flagged for bottom-edge truncation remain included. The exclusion breakdown is in Supporting Table S1.

Six representations are compared: STROKE, HC-183, frozen ImageNet ResNet-18, EfficientNet-B0 and ViT-B/16 embeddings \cite{he2016resnet,tan2019efficientnet,dosovitskiy2021vit}, and Stroke-CNN. No network is trained on these writers. The following specifications define the descriptors and checkpoints used throughout the evaluation.

\subsection{Preprocessing and descriptor construction}
All extraction is implemented in \texttt{code/wid\_features.py}, \texttt{hc183.py} and \texttt{stroke\_features.py}. Feature matrices are supplied so that the identification and control analyses can be reproduced without rerunning neural inference.

\textbf{HC-183.} A photograph is converted to grayscale and resized to width 768 with aspect ratio preserved using Lanczos interpolation. For grayscale intensity $I$ on the 0--255 scale, a Gaussian background estimate $B$ with standard deviation 17 pixels defines ink by $(B-I>17)\land(B>120)\land(I<205)$. A four-pixel border is cleared and a $2\times2$ binary opening is applied. Gradients of the mask smoothed with standard deviation 0.7 give nine-bin unsigned orientation histograms and ink density in each of $4\times4$ cells (160 values). Row and column ink projections each contribute seven quantiles (0, .1, .25, .5, .75, .9, 1), mean and standard deviation (18 values). Five area quantiles (.1, .25, .5, .75, .9) of connected components with at least three pixels complete the 183 features.

\textbf{STROKE.} The corresponding ink rule is evaluated at width 1,536, with background smoothing standard deviation 34, an eight-pixel cleared border and removal of connected objects smaller than 12 pixels. The text block is cropped using row and column ink fractions (thresholds 0.01 and 0.005), their 0.5th/99.5th percentiles and ten pixels of padding. Stroke width is twice the mean distance-transform value on the skeleton. A contour sampling length $L=\max(4,\operatorname{round}(2\,\mathrm{width}))$ controls feature scale. The 208 components comprise a 16-bin contour-direction histogram, a $12\times12$ hinge histogram, 12 turning-angle bins, 12 endpoint-direction bins, 12 skeleton-orientation bins, nine slant bins, and endpoint, junction and hole counts normalized by skeleton length. Static contours do not reveal pen movement order. The mask discards grayscale information, but this alone does not prove that every acquisition effect is removed.

\textbf{Frozen grayscale embeddings.} Grayscale images are resized to width 1,568 using Lanczos interpolation; the tile-selection mask is the nearest-neighbor resize of the 768-pixel HC-183 mask. Nonoverlapping $224\times224$ tiles with at least 1\% ink are retained; the bottom edge is padded with median grayscale intensity. If no tile meets the threshold, all tiles are used. Grayscale is repeated into three channels and normalized by ImageNet means $(.485,.456,.406)$ and standard deviations $(.229,.224,.225)$. The checkpoints are torchvision \texttt{ResNet18\_Weights.IMAGENET1K\_V1}, \texttt{EfficientNet\_B0\_Weights.IMAGENET1K\_V1} and \texttt{ViT\_B\_16\_Weights.IMAGENET1K\_V1}; final classification layers are removed. Mean tile embeddings have dimensions 512, 1,280 and 768. Models remain in evaluation mode without fine-tuning.

\textbf{Stroke-CNN.} The frozen ResNet-18 above instead receives black-on-white binary tiles from the cropped high-resolution mask. Bilinear resizing targets a stroke width of three pixels, with image dimensions at least 224 pixels; interpolation can produce antialiased edges. White padding completes the tile grid, and tiles require at least 2\% ink; if none qualify, all are retained. Input normalization and mean aggregation follow the grayscale embeddings. This representation has 512 components. Its name does not indicate a separately trained handwriting network.

\textbf{Adjusted HC-183.} Seven grayscale statistics (mean, standard deviation and .05/.25/.5/.75/.95 quantiles), together with mask ink density, form nuisance covariates. A linear regression with intercept removes their fitted contribution from HC-183. Regression coefficients are fitted within each outer or inner SVM training split, then applied to its training and held-out samples. The adjusted representation is a sensitivity check, not a guarantee of causal removal of acquisition confounding.

\subsection{Gallery construction and identity matching}

Identification is closed-set over all writers. A shared-prompt query is compared with the other five prompts of every writer. Self-chosen-text queries are compared with every subset of $k=1,2,3$ or 6 shared reference paragraphs. For each reference configuration, feature means and standard deviations are fitted \emph{only to its selected whole-paragraph gallery references}. The resulting transform is applied to references and queries. Each transformed vector is $L_2$-normalized; a gallery template is the normalized mean of that writer's reference vectors. Cosine similarity $s_w$ supplies the baseline ranking. Query pages and unused reference pages do not fit this transform. Four query sizes, five reference conditions and six representations give 120 settings.

Table~\ref{tab:mainnesting} separates the closed identification gallery from the writer-held-out attribute-training data. Holding out a writer for attribute prediction does not remove that writer from the identity gallery.

\subsection{Handedness prediction and score fusion}
For each query writer and unit size, a linear SVM ($C=0.1$) is trained on valid shared units of the other 41 writers using HC-183. Standardization is fitted within training data. Five-fold stratified group cross-validation over those training writers supplies out-of-fold scores for logistic calibration \cite{platt1999probabilistic}. The outer writer's samples and labels are excluded from both stages. Let $p_q=\sigma(\ell_q)$ be the resulting left-hand probability. Adjusted HC-183 and STROKE predictors provide sensitivity checks.

These are cross-validated performance estimates. Writer identity assigns evaluation folds; it is not an input available to a deployed predictor. Deployment would freeze one attribute model and its calibration, temperature and fusion weight using separate development writers, then score every future query without choosing a model by its unknown identity. Training on the enrolled writers instead defines a different target. We do not estimate performance of one universal deployed predictor here.

In the \emph{known} scenario, both query and gallery hands are supplied. This oracle restricts the top-ranked group to the query's true hand. In the primary \emph{predicted-query} scenario, gallery hands are known, and $p_q$ is predicted. Probabilistic score fusion ranks writers by
\begin{equation}
 f_w=s_w/T+\log p_q(h_w),
 \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
where $p_q(1)=p_q$ and $p_q(0)=1-p_q$. For each setting, $T$ is selected from 31 logarithmically spaced values between $10^{-3}$ and 1 by minimizing identification negative log loss on the other query writers, with equal writer weights. Temperature fitting uses identity scores and writer targets, not attribute scores or the held-out query writer's outcomes. Gallery references remain available because this is closed-set identification.

Equation~\eqref{eq:fusion} is a score-combination rule. It has a Bayesian interpretation only under additional assumptions about the dependence of its evidence and the calibration prior. We do not assume these hold for two representations of the same handwriting. Hard filtering instead ranks all writers with the predicted hand ahead of the other hand, preserving identity-score order within each group. In this balanced gallery, an incorrect hand prediction makes top-1 success impossible.

\subsection{Nested fusion-weight comparison}
\label{sec:nested}
We additionally rank candidates by $s_w/T+\lambda\log p_q(h_w)$, selecting a nonnegative weight for each held-out query writer. The other 41 writers supply five development folds, regenerating attribute probabilities inside the outer exclusion to avoid selection bias \cite{cawley2010selection}. Each development attribute model and its inner five-fold writer-group calibrator exclude both the outer writer and the entire development fold. Equal-writer development negative log loss selects $\lambda\in\{0,.1,.2,.3,.5,.7,1,1.5,2,3,5\}$ at the same ID-only temperature as fixed fusion. Final query predictions use the outer model trained on the other 41 writers. The grid includes no attribute evidence ($\lambda=0$) and the fixed rule ($\lambda=1$); exact ties select the smaller weight. Known gallery metadata remain available in development and evaluation. The supplementary methods specify the complete nested fitting hierarchy.

\subsection{Filtering identity and controls}
\label{sec:identity}
Let $C$ denote correct attribute prediction and $K$ successful identification with the true query hand supplied. With both groups nonempty, filtered success is $C\cap K$. Thus
\begin{align}
 A_{\mathrm{filter}}&=aP(K\mid C)\nonumber\\
 &=aA_{\mathrm{known}}+\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf1_C,\mathbf1_K),
 \label{eq:exact}
\end{align}
where $a=P(C)$. Only if the two correctness events are independent does this reduce to
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 A_{\mathrm{filter}}&\approx aA_{\mathrm{known}},\\
 a_{\mathrm{break}}&\approx A_{\mathrm{none}}/A_{\mathrm{known}}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:approx}
\end{equation}
We measure the conditional accuracies and approximation residuals rather than treating the product as universal. The two marginal accuracies alone permit the bounds
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \max(0,a+A_{\mathrm{known}}-1)&\leq A_{\mathrm{filter}},\\
 A_{\mathrm{filter}}&\leq\min(a,A_{\mathrm{known}}).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:bounds}
\end{equation}

Known balanced random writer partitions test how much oracle benefit comes from supplying an arbitrary correct partition. With 21 writers in each group, a query whose true writer is outranked by $j$ impostors survives an oracle filter only when all $j$ are assigned to the opposite group. Uniform balanced partitions therefore give the exact expectation
\begin{equation}
P(\mathrm{success}\mid j)=\begin{cases}\binom{21}{j}/\binom{41}{j},&0\leq j\leq21,\\0,&j>21.\end{cases}
\label{eq:randompartition}
\end{equation}
The same deterministic tie precedence is used for baseline ranks and partition success. The writer-weighted expectation provides an exact benchmark; 2,000 common Monte Carlo partitions characterize partition-to-partition variation across settings. Partition quantiles are not independent-cohort confidence intervals. Separately, 20 nearly hand-balanced random 21/21 writer labelings test whether an attribute model can predict arbitrary writer-associated labels. For each labeling, the positive group contains 10 or 11 of the 21 left-handed writers. Writer-held-out prediction is compared with enrolment-trained prediction, where other paragraphs of the same writer are available. The latter is an explicitly different generalization target; it is not a causal decomposition of genuine handedness accuracy.

\subsection{Endpoints and inferential scope}
\label{sec:statistics}
Top-1 accuracy is averaged over valid query units and reference configurations within each writer, then equally over writers. Across-setting means give each of the 120 settings equal weight. Paired differences use 9,999 writer-level sign flips and 95\% intervals from 10,000 hand-stratified writer bootstraps. The same sampled writer indices are used across settings for aggregate intervals, preserving their dependence. These intervals condition on the observed gallery and fitted predictions; they do not include new-gallery sampling or full model-refitting uncertainty.

Four settings were designated after an exploratory feasibility analysis of paragraph-level conditions: ResNet-18 and ViT-B/16 on self-chosen paragraphs with one reference and quarter strips with six references. These analyses were not preregistered. Holm correction covers the four fixed-fusion comparisons. Learned-weight comparisons against no fusion and fixed fusion form eight exploratory contrasts (four settings, two comparators), with Holm correction across all eight. Other robustness analyses are descriptive. No equivalence margin was specified, so nonsignificance is not interpreted as absence of a useful effect.


\subsection{External validation across capture sessions}
\label{sec:csafemethods}
The CSAFE Handwriting Database provides an independent English-language evaluation \cite{crawford2020database,crawford2026csafe}. Its version-2 release contains 475 writers; this differs from the 90-writer release described in the original article. The released survey identifies 48 left-handed, 423 right-handed, three ambidextrous and one unreported-hand writer. We include all 48 left-handed writers and 48 right-handed writers sampled with seed 20261108. Each selected writer contributes six session-1 pages (London Letter, LND, and Wizard of Oz, WOZ, each repeated three times) for auxiliary-model training. One LND page is the sole identity-gallery reference. Queries are separate session-2 pages using LND or WOZ, plus a session-3 WOZ page for secondary evaluation. Thus the primary conditions distinguish matching prompts from cross-prompt identification across sessions.

The original whole-unit extraction rules, SVM $C=0.1$, checkpoints and fusion grids are retained. We evaluate ResNet-18, ViT-B/16, HC-183 and STROKE identity features, with HC-183 as the primary handedness predictor and the two original sensitivity predictors. All 864 selected pages pass the validity rules. Each outer query writer's six training pages are excluded from its attribute model and calibrator. Nested development probabilities additionally exclude their validation writers; temperature and weight targets exclude the outer query writer. Known gallery references and hands remain permitted metadata. Numerical score scaling preserves the original logistic-calibration objective; Supporting Section S13 documents the convergence check and its implementation amendment.

Eight primary contrasts compare fixed and nested fusion with identity only for ResNet-18 and ViT-B/16 in both session-2 conditions, with Holm correction across all eight. Session 3, alternate attribute models and 20 refitted balanced 21/21 galleries are secondary checks. Confidence intervals condition on the fitted models and enrolled gallery. The CSAFE design was specified before inspecting its performance outcomes; it is an extension of the existing study, not a globally preregistered dataset-sampling plan. This is within-dataset refitting on an external cohort, not transfer of a frozen Bangla-trained predictor.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Known and predicted query handedness}
The primary predictor attains 68.7\% accuracy on shared paragraphs and 65.5\% on self-chosen paragraphs; averaging paragraph log-odds within a writer gives 73.8\% over all eight paragraphs. The latter uses more writing and is not the accuracy available for a single query. Sensitivity predictors and strip accuracies appear in the supplement. For self-chosen paragraphs, the primary predictor has Brier score 0.204 and negative log loss 0.602; the reliability plots and intervals do not isolate miscalibration as the cause of fusion failure.

Known handedness improves top-1 accuracy by 6.1 percentage points on average (95\% conditional interval [+5.1, +7.1]). Probabilistic score fusion changes it by -0.6 points [-2.3, +0.9], and hard filtering by -14.3 points [-19.3, -9.5]. Table~\ref{tab:main} shows two representative reference conditions. Differences between representations are substantial, so an across-setting mean should not be interpreted as the expected accuracy of an arbitrary deployed system. Figure~\ref{fig:performance} shows the effects of query size and reference count.

\subsection{Conditional filtering utility}
Across 456 combinations of setting and prediction model, the independence product tracks filtering accuracy ($r=0.996$), with mean absolute error 1.5 points and signed bias -0.9 points. These combinations reuse writers and are not independent replications. The residual analyses in Fig.~\ref{fig:diagnostics} and supplementary conditional accuracies show why the exact identity in Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact} is preferable when predictions are available. For HC-183 identification on self-chosen paragraphs with one reference, the product predicts 31.4\% versus 28.0\% observed. Known-hand identification succeeds on 42.7\% of queries with correct hand predictions and 58.0\% with incorrect predictions. Agreement over a broad performance range can therefore conceal decision-relevant residuals.

For self-chosen queries, the approximate break-even accuracy has median 86\% and range 55--98\%. This is a property of the tested identifiers and reference conditions under Eq.~\eqref{eq:approx}, not a general handedness requirement. A stronger baseline leaves less room for an imperfect filter to help. A known balanced random partition improves accuracy by 6.9 points on average. Known handedness differs from that expectation by -0.8 points (95\% conditional interval [-1.8, +0.2]). Thus the oracle benefit is consistent with useful partition information, without a distinctive advantage over the random-partition benchmark.

\subsection{Decision margins and opposing fusion effects}
Fusion preserves candidate ranking within each handedness group. Define the true-writer margin against the best opposite-hand candidate and the signed attribute log-odds as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}m_q&=\frac{s_{w^*}-\max_{h_w\ne h_{w^*}}s_w}{T},\\r_q&=\log\frac{p_q(h_{w^*})}{p_q(1-h_{w^*})}.\end{aligned}
\label{eq:decisionmargin}
\end{equation}
Away from score ties, identification succeeds exactly when the true writer leads its own hand group and $m_q+\lambda r_q>0$. Thus fusion cannot repair a wrong within-group ordering. The true identity and handedness enter only this retrospective diagnostic; they are not available inputs to the operational query predictor.

A rescued decision is incorrect before fusion and correct afterwards; a damaged decision changes in the opposite direction. Their difference equals the net top-1 change. Across the 120 settings, fixed fusion rescues 2.02\% of decisions and damages 2.59\%; nested weighting rescues 1.33\% and damages 1.42\%. Figure~\ref{fig:mainmargins} shows these opposing effects with conditional writer-bootstrap intervals and illustrates the exact margin boundary for HC-183 paragraph queries with one reference.

Direct candidate-score ranking and the analytical decision reconstruction agree on all 664,680 evaluated query/reference decisions, with no exact boundary ties. Writer-level fused accuracies independently match the fixed and nested outputs. This deterministic consistency check explains the fitted score rule; it does not add independent experimental observations.

\section{Discussion}
The methodological lesson is that oracle gains and attribute accuracy do not establish downstream identification utility. Correct metadata reveal an opportunity to improve ranking; an attribute predictor must still supply useful evidence at the decisions where it matters. In BanglaHand, neither calibrated fixed fusion nor properly nested learned weighting establishes a gain in the designated comparisons. The CSAFE validation extends this distinction to a separate population, English writing and distinct source pages and sessions: even the 67.71\% same-prompt handedness predictor yields no reliably demonstrated primary fusion gain. The cross-prompt and session-3 differences show why this result must remain tied to its declared query conditions and fitted rules. The random-partition benchmark further shows that the oracle advantage is consistent with correct binary partition information, without a distinctive handedness advantage. Hard filtering turns an incorrect hand prediction into an unrecoverable top-1 error. Soft fusion avoids that veto, but calibration alone cannot ensure that overlapping evidence will improve the identity ranking.

Table~\ref{tab:positioning} makes the methodological comparison explicit. Prior attribute studies establish whether handwriting supports demographic prediction, while identity-focused studies develop the matcher itself. Our contribution is to intervene on the auxiliary information while holding the identity comparison fixed. The known-hand and random-partition controls quantify an opportunity that a predictor may fail to realize; nested weighting then tests whether a flexible, calibrated use of that predictor changes the finding. This is a complementary evaluation question, not evidence that our representations are superior to the recognition systems in Table~\ref{tab:related}.

The interaction between confidence and identity margins determines which decisions fusion changes. The decision-level analysis in the Results distinguishes rescued identifications from damaged ones and verifies the exact ranking boundary.

Predictable handedness \cite{prappo2026handedness} is compatible with these outcomes: attribute errors can erase oracle gains, and attribute cues may duplicate identity evidence. Our fusion results do not imply that handedness is unidentifiable.

Nested weighting reduces the mean loss relative to fixed fusion. Since the weight minimizes development log loss, this result concerns that explicit selection objective rather than an exhaustive search for top-1-optimal fusion. The study does not identify a universal attribute-accuracy threshold for soft fusion. The filtering identity serves as a diagnostic of the joint correctness distribution; its product approximation requires independence. Even equal marginal accuracies need not imply equal utility. Likewise, high attribute accuracy on enrolled writers may be appropriate for a declared closed-set application, while overstating performance on unseen writers. The random-label control diagnoses this distinction without assigning a causal fraction to identity.




The practical implication is a testable evaluation sequence. An auxiliary-attribute system should first establish an oracle opportunity, then compare predicted fusion with an identity-only baseline on the same queries, selecting its calibration and fusion parameters without the evaluated writer's outcomes. Decision-level rescues and damage show where the score change helps, while writer-overlap controls distinguish an attribute-generalization question from an enrolment-conditioned one. Separate capture sessions then test whether the conclusion survives a change in source pages and acquisition. Our controls make these questions answerable even when the final fusion gain is inconclusive. Their broader value is an auditable decision about whether to deploy an auxiliary score, rather than treating attribute accuracy as a proxy for identification benefit.

\section{Scope and limitations}
BanglaHand provides a deliberately balanced cohort of 21 left- and 21 right-handed writers under controlled acquisition \cite{banglahand2026data}. Recruiting the left-handed group required substantial effort; this balance enables comparisons without majority-class dominance. The estimates describe this 42-writer closed gallery from one region, device and photographic session. The companion study shares this cohort and is not independent validation. CSAFE supplies an independent population and separate-session scans, extending the evaluation beyond the original photographic acquisition; it does not establish transfer to all scripts, devices or deployment conditions. Each writer contributes two self-chosen paragraphs, and strips vary text quantity without adding independent writers.

The uncertainty intervals condition on the enrolled gallery and fitted predictions; they do not propagate full model-refitting or population-sampling variability. BanglaHand calibration partitions vary one fitting stage and writer omission changes evaluation averaging while retaining its gallery. CSAFE tests new sessions and refits models in overlapping gallery subsamples, but those dependent subsamples do not provide independent-cohort confidence intervals. Its whole-page, one-reference conditions do not reproduce every BanglaHand text-size/reference setting, and same-prompt matching must be distinguished from cross-prompt identification.

The computational comparison uses handcrafted descriptors and frozen ImageNet representations, allowing reproducible evaluation without task-specific network training. Handwriting-specialized training, fine-tuning, stronger identifiers and alternative fusion objectives remain outside this comparison; their different error patterns or margins could change fusion utility. Handedness is the sole real auxiliary attribute tested. BDSHWA records handedness for Bengali--English online handwriting but includes one left-handed participant \cite{bdshwa2026}, so it cannot supply a balanced offline replication.

The primary predicted-query protocol assumes reliable gallery handedness. Knowing a suspect's handedness does not establish the hand of the questioned writer. The known scenario assumes both are reliably available. Closed-set top-1 ranking does not evaluate open-set rejection, evidential likelihood ratios, disguised writing, or non-dominant-hand writing. Application to forensic candidate prioritization would require validation under those operational conditions and explicit treatment of uncertain attributes and error costs. Predicted handedness alone cannot justify exclusion.

\section{Conclusions}
The controlled comparisons show why oracle gains and attribute accuracy cannot substitute for measured identification utility. On BanglaHand, correct query and gallery handedness improves writer ranking, whereas fixed and properly nested learned-weight fusion of the tested query predictor produce no reliably demonstrated gain over identification alone, and hard filtering causes substantial losses. Independent CSAFE writers and capture sessions reproduce the distinction between oracle opportunity and achieved predicted-attribute utility in the tested comparisons. The analytical filtering diagnostic connects success to joint correctness, while the oracle, predicted-attribute and random-label controls distinguish different sources of apparent utility. Random-label prediction shows that enrolled-writer attribute accuracy can contain substantial writer-associated information. Further datasets, fitting objectives and deployment conditions can assess the remaining limits of transfer; the present results do not establish a universal absence of handedness utility. Future fusion methods should be evaluated on downstream utility, calibration and error dependence, with the query/gallery information available at deployment stated explicitly.
